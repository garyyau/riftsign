# Quiz question design research (2026-09-23)

Compiled by a Sonnet 5 research agent. Question: two early testers disagreed on Match accuracy (one felt spot-on, one didn't); how should Riftsign write its ~25 scenario/statement Questions to measure real playstyle preference more accurately? Claims are cited inline; anything not traced to a fetched or well-corroborated source is marked unverified (†).

## 1. Item formats and trade-offs

| Format | Best for | Main bias risk |
|---|---|---|
| Likert / agree-disagree | Fast to write, easy to answer, good for clear attitudes | Acquiescence ("yea-saying"), central tendency, extreme responding ([Wikipedia: Likert scale response biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likert_scale); [GESIS response-bias guidelines](https://www.gesis.org/fileadmin/admin/Dateikatalog/pdf/guidelines/response_biases_standardized_surveys_bogner_landrock_2016.pdf)) |
| Forced-choice / ipsative | Reducing acquiescence and social-desirability; faking-resistant selection contexts | Ipsative scores aren't directly comparable across people unless modeled with Thurstonian IRT ([Frontiers: forced-choice vs. Likert](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6803422/); [TIRT development paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11673971/)) |
| Situational judgment (scenario) | Concrete, realistic decisions; moderate criterion validity (pooled r≈0.32) | Test-takers may pick the option that looks "correct" or skilled rather than the one they'd actually choose; video/realistic framing outperforms flat text ([SJT meta-analysis, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0211884)) |
| Behavioral-frequency ("how often do you…") | Narrow, concrete behaviors; less abstract than trait self-description | Still self-report; framing that normalizes the behavior reduces social-desirability distortion ([face-saving item wording study](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jssam/smaf017/8307588)) |
| Preference/ranking, MaxDiff | Forcing real trade-offs between things that all sound appealing | None of the "everything is important" flattening that plain ratings produce ([MaxDiff overview](https://www.qualtrics.com/articles/strategy-research/an-introduction-to-maxdiff/)) |
| Semantic differential / slider | Continuous bipolar traits (a natural fit for an Axis model) | Direction bias if the "positive" pole always sits on the same side; vary it ([Osgood semantic differential, Sage Methods](https://methods.sagepub.com/reference/encyclopedia-of-survey-research-methods/n527.xml)) |
| Image/example-based | Reducing reading burden, working well when the domain has concrete visual artifacts (cards) | Validated to perform similarly to text items for the Big Five when forced-choice ([image-based Big Five validation, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8883940/)) |

Riftsign's "scenario" kind is already close to a forced-choice/SJT hybrid; its "statement" kind is plain Likert and carries the acquiescence/central-tendency risk documented above.

## 2. Item-writing rules

- **Specificity vs. genericness**: this is the bandwidth-fidelity trade-off (Cronbach & Gleser, 1957) — broad items predict broad outcomes only moderately well, narrow/concrete items predict narrow, concrete outcomes best ([bandwidth-fidelity dilemma summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315833294_Bandwidth-Fidelity_Dilemma)). A quiz trying to predict a concrete choice (which Legend to play) should lean toward concrete scenario framing over abstract self-description.
- **Double-barreled items**: don't fold two ideas into one item; if a respondent could agree with half and disagree with half, split it ([DeVellis 2016, via CultureMonkey summary](https://www.culturemonkey.io/employee-engagement/double-barreled-survey-question/); [Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/methodology/double-barreled-question/)).
- **Reverse-keying**: intended to counter acquiescence, but reverse-worded items often form their own spurious "method factor" driven by negation wording rather than the trait itself, and confuse respondents, especially under time pressure ([PLOS One: reverse items and factor structure](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0157795); ["cows in the rain" study, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3729568/)). Net effect on short scales is frequently negative — balanced keying (equal items favoring each pole) is safer than negated wording.
- **Items per trait**: minimum 3–4 per axis for a stable score; 4–6 is the sweet spot; below 3 the score is unstable ([Wiley, HRM scale-length guidance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hrm.21852)) — already the standard used in `docs/research/2026-09-20-scoring-models.md`.
- **Cross-loading items**: an item loading on more than one construct dilutes each construct's clarity unless the overlap is theoretically deliberate ([cross-loading discussion, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/post/EFA-cross-loading-items-and-reliability-analyses)). Deliberate cross-loading (one Answer moving several Axes) is standard in bifactor-style designs, but every Axis still needs some items that load on it alone, or its score is just a shadow of the other Axes it's bundled with.
- **Balanced, equally attractive options; no "right answer"**: SJTs lose validity when one option reads as the objectively better play; face-saving, normalized wording ("many players find X hard…") measurably reduces the pull toward the "good" answer ([face-saving wording study](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jssam/smaf017/8307588)). Randomizing/balancing which side of an option list carries the "skilled-sounding" phrasing also helps.

## 3. Stated vs. revealed preference

- General finding: revealed-preference (actual choice) data predicts real-world outcomes noticeably better than the best stated-preference technique in comparative studies, though the size of the gap is domain-dependent and stated preference can outperform for predicting future *stated* intentions specifically ([stated vs. revealed preference summary](https://www.dectech.co.uk/news-insights/buzz/how-to-ask-why-stated-versus-revealed-preference-research/); [health-economics stated/revealed bias study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hec.4246)).
- Game-specific evidence: Quantic Foundry's Nick Yee reports that when they tried scoring World of Warcraft players on the Big Five (OCEAN), "the strongest OCEAN trait predicted <5% of player behavior" measured from telemetry — generic self-reported personality traits were a weak predictor of actual play, which is why they built a game-specific, behavior-grounded motivation model instead ([Nick Yee interview, Player Driven](https://playerdriven.substack.com/p/quantic-foundrys-nick-yee-on-gamer)).
- Broader game-research trend: telemetry (revealed behavior) is increasingly preferred over self-report for measuring play patterns because self-reports of digital behavior are described as "notoriously imprecise and biased" (†, summarized via search snippet of a Royal Society Open Science paper; direct fetch was blocked, so treat as corroborating rather than confirmed).
- Implication: a concrete forced-choice item ("which of these two openings would you keep") is structurally closer to a revealed-preference measurement than a generic statement ("I like long games") and should predict deck-choice behavior better.

## 4. Gamer and TCG precedents

- **Quantic Foundry's Gamer Motivation Profile**: built from a literature review, an initial item pool tested on 1,127 gamers and validated on another 600, refined via factor analysis into a small number of motivation clusters, later validated against data from tens of thousands and eventually over a million respondents ([search-synthesized from Quantic Foundry's own posts](https://quanticfoundry.com/2015/06/18/how-we-created-the-gamer-motivation-profile/), [FAQ](https://quanticfoundry.com/faq/) — both blocked direct fetch, so treated as secondary). Items are deliberately phrased around grounded, jargon-free everyday behaviors rather than gaming terminology, and results have been validated against studios' own backend behavioral metrics, not just self-report agreement (Yee interview, above).
- **Bartle's taxonomy** (Achiever/Explorer/Socializer/Killer): originated as a 1996 essay on MUD players, not an empirically factor-analyzed instrument at the time; commonly criticized for treating fluid, context-dependent motivations as fixed, mutually exclusive categories, and for lacking quantitative validation of the categories themselves — though Bartle's own model is arguably closer to axes than the discrete "types" popular culture treats it as ([Bartle taxonomy overview and critique, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartle_taxonomy_of_player_types)).
- **MTG Timmy/Johnny/Spike**: Mark Rosewater's 2002 psychographic profiles, built for internal design use, not a validated psychometric tool. The profiles are camps a player is assigned to, not measured as a spectrum ([Rosewater, "The Three Magic Psychographics"](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-three-magic-psychographics)).
- **MTG Vorthos/Mel**: originally framed the same way (opposite ends of one axis), but Rosewater later corrected this once he found cards that appealed strongly to both at once: "Vorthos and Mel each exist on their own scale. You are not Vorthos or Mel but rather Vorthos or anti-Vorthos and Mel or anti-Mel" ([Rosewater, "Vorthos and Mel"](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/vorthos-and-mel-2015-08-31)). This is a direct, sourced lesson that two named psychographic camps should not be assumed to trade off against each other unless there's real evidence they do.
- **MBTI vs. Big Five**: MBTI forces continuous preference scores into a binary per dichotomy, even though most people score near the middle; up to 50% of retakers get a different four-letter type after five weeks; predictive validity for real-world outcomes (e.g. job performance) is weak to none, versus consistently stronger validity for the continuous Big Five traits. Type labels remain more "engaging and memorable" than trait scores despite being less accurate ([MBTI vs. Big Five reliability/validity summary](https://www.earlyyears.tv/mbti-vs-big-five-personality-test/); [MBTI validity critique](https://jobcannon.io/methodology/mbti/validity)). This is the standard cautionary tale for any quiz — like Riftsign's — that ultimately collapses continuous scores into one named result.

## 5. Short-test accuracy

- Adaptive testing (CAT) can hold measurement precision with roughly half the items of a fixed test, and can identify respondents who are extreme on a trait with near-perfect accuracy using as little as 25% of a full item pool ([computerized adaptive personality assessment study, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2614658/); [narcissism CAT study, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01437/full)). Full IRT-based branching is likely overkill for a ~25-item fan quiz, but the underlying principle — spend extra questions resolving the cases that are actually ambiguous — is transferable without building a full adaptive engine.
- MaxDiff-style trade-off items ("which matters more") are more discriminating than independent ratings because every choice yields both a positive and a negative preference signal, and they eliminate the "everything matters" flattening effect of rating scales ([MaxDiff overview](https://www.qualtrics.com/articles/strategy-research/an-introduction-to-maxdiff/)). This maps naturally onto Axes that are already framed as opposing poles.
- Collapsing a continuous preference into a small number of Likert categories loses real variance that a slider or finer scale would keep ([information loss in Likert responses, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0271949)) — relevant if Riftsign ever wants a "how strongly" dimension on statement items rather than plain agree/disagree.
- Detecting an inconsistent or all-middle respondent: the careless-responding literature recommends combining several signals — straightlining/longstring detection (repeated identical or alternating answers), completion-time outliers, and consistency checks between similar items — rather than relying on any single indicator ([careless-responding best practices, Annual Review of Psychology](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-040422-045007?crawler=true); [straightlining detection, Current Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-026-09186-3)). For Riftsign specifically, a practical proxy without dedicated attention-check items is to watch for Answers that repeatedly move an Axis in contradictory directions, or a Profile whose raw Axis movements cluster near zero across most Axes — both are signs the Match should be reported with lower confidence rather than the same certainty as a Player who answered consistently.

## 6. Perceived accuracy (kept separate from real measurement accuracy)

- The Barnum/Forer effect: Bertram Forer gave 39 psychology students an identical, generic personality vignette lifted from an astrology text and told them it was individually tailored; they rated its accuracy 4.3 out of 5 on average, and the finding has replicated widely since ([Wikipedia: Barnum effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)). The effect is strongest when the description is believed to be personalized, is framed by a perceived authority, skews positive/flattering, and uses hedge language ("at times," "you sometimes") that lets the reader supply their own meaning (same source).
- This means a quiz result can *feel* accurate for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the underlying Axis math is right — the friendly tester's positive reaction is not, by itself, evidence the scoring model is sound, any more than the unhappy tester's reaction disproves it. Both need to be checked against what the Axis answers actually computed.
- "Showing your reasoning" and "surfacing a runner-up result" as levers for perceived accuracy are plausible based on the Barnum mechanism (specific, traceable reasoning is the opposite of vague, unfalsifiable flattery) but no primary study of quiz result formats was found to confirm this directly — marked unverified (†).

## Implications for Riftsign

1. Keep the scenario Question as the backbone format — it already behaves like a forced-choice/SJT hybrid, which resists acquiescence and social-desirability bias better than plain agree/disagree statements (§1).
2. For statement-kind Questions, don't rely only on the `reverse` flag to counter acquiescence: negated reverse-keyed wording can create a spurious factor of its own and confuse readers. Prefer balanced keying — write enough separate statement items that agreement pulls each Axis in both directions across the item set, rather than negating the same statement (§2).
3. Keep the existing 3–4-item-per-Axis floor; don't let any Axis drop to 1–2 contributing Answers even after cross-loading is counted (§2).
4. Deliberate cross-loading (an Answer moving several Axes, already used, e.g. `opening-hand` moving both pace and stance) is fine, but every Axis needs some Answers that load on it alone — otherwise its score is just a shadow of whatever Axis it's always bundled with (§2).
5. Favor a concrete, in-the-moment framing over generic self-description for any new statement item: self-reported traits are a documented weak predictor of actual play behavior (the OCEAN-vs-WoW-telemetry finding, §3), so "when my opponent stabilizes, I…" measures more than "I am a patient player."
6. Make sure every Answer in a scenario Question reads as an equally legitimate, skilled playstyle. SJT validity drops when one option reads as the objectively "correct" or more skilled play — that pulls answers toward social desirability instead of real preference (§1, §2).
7. Since the Profile ultimately collapses into a single nearest Legend, expect the same "near-tie flip" failure mode that hits MBTI and 16Personalities at their dichotomy midpoints (§4). Consider whether a Player whose Profile sits within noise distance of two Builds should see that reflected (e.g. a close runner-up) rather than one Match presented with false certainty — this is a design inference from the MBTI evidence, not a directly sourced claim.
8. Take the Vorthos/Mel correction as a standing reminder: don't assume two things trade off on one Axis unless there's real evidence for it. Riftsign's Domain Axes are already structurally bipolar because Riftbound prints Domains as fixed opposite pairs, so this is a fit worth re-checking rather than a currently known problem (§4).
9. Keep following the jargon-avoidance rule already in `CONTEXT.md` — it matches what the most-validated gamer-motivation instrument in the industry does deliberately: phrase every item around a concrete, everyday moment, never a category label like "tempo" or "aggro" (§4).
10. Add a lightweight consistency signal even without formal attention-check items: flag (at least internally) any completed Profile whose raw Axis movements land in a narrow band around zero across most Axes, or whose Answers repeatedly pull one Axis in contradictory directions — that Player's Match is less trustworthy than one from a Player who answered consistently, which may explain why the two early testers disagreed (§5).
11. If a faster or higher-precision version of the test is ever wanted, don't try to hand-build a full adaptive engine; a low-tech approximation of the same idea is to spend any extra Questions resolving Players who land close to a Legend-dividing boundary rather than adding Questions evenly for everyone (§5).
12. For the Questions that most separate the leading Legend candidates, consider a MaxDiff-style forced trade-off between two Axis poles instead of independent statement agreement — it's more discriminating and matches the fact that Axes are already framed as opposing poles (§1, §5).
13. Treat "a tester said their Match felt right" and "a tester disagreed" as two data points about perceived accuracy, not measurement accuracy — the Barnum effect means flattering, vague results get rated as accurate regardless of whether the scoring is right, so don't tune Legend descriptions to be more flattering or vague as a substitute for checking the Axis math (§6).
14. A card-image forced-choice format ("which of these two cards would you rather open with") is a validated item type and a natural fit for a card game; it sits closer to revealed preference than a written statement and is worth prototyping if the team wants to compare against the current scenario-Question wording (§1, §3).
